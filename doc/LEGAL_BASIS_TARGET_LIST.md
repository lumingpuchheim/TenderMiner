# Legal basis for the target list

Research against primary sources — the GDPR text, the German supervisory
authorities' joint guidance (DSK), and the EDPB — for the question left open in
`ONBOARDING.md` §2.1 (the **[CLARIFY]** at the channel section) and §7 decision
#1: does holding and using the outreach target list, built from contact details
in published procurement notices, need **consent**, or does **Art. 6(1)(f)
legitimate interest** carry it? The channel under examination is postal letters
only; cold e-mail is already ruled out by §7 Abs. 2 UWG and is not revisited
here.

Sources are the regulation itself ([Regulation (EU) 2016/679, via
gdpr-info.eu](https://gdpr-info.eu/)), the DSK [Orientierungshilfe zur
Verarbeitung von personenbezogenen Daten für Zwecke der Direktwerbung, Februar
2022](https://www.datenschutzkonferenz-online.de/media/oh/OH-Werbung_Februar%202022_final.pdf)
("DSK OH", cited by its section numbers), and the EDPB [Guidelines 1/2024 on
processing of personal data based on Article 6(1)(f)
GDPR](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2024-10/edpb_guidelines_202401_legitimateinterest_en.pdf)
(version for public consultation, adopted 8 October 2024; cited by paragraph).
Where the UWG matters, [§7 UWG](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/uwg_2004/__7.html)
is cited directly.

## Verdict

**No, consent is not required — Art. 6(1)(f) is the workable legal basis for a
postal-only B2B list — but the "no" is conditional, and one DSK passage cuts
against the comfortable version of it.**

The conditions the "no" rests on:

1. The channel stays postal. Letters are outside the ePrivacy consent regime
   and outside §7 Abs. 2 UWG's consent list; e-mail and (toward consumers)
   phone are not.
2. A **documented legitimate-interest assessment** (three-step test) exists
   before the first batch, and it honestly works through the weakest point:
   the contact details were published because procurement law requires it, not
   voluntarily — the DSK has held exactly that fact against marketing use of
   Impressum data (DSK OH 4.2). The distinguishing arguments exist (below,
   finding 3) but a lawyer has to weigh them, which is what ONBOARDING.md
   decision #1 already says.
3. The **Art. 14 information notice travels with the first letter** — a
   landing-page notice alone does not satisfy the timing rule.
4. The **Art. 21(2) objection is honoured absolutely and forever** — the
   "do not contact" ledger flag is not a courtesy, it is the load-bearing
   safeguard the whole balancing leans on.
5. **No profiling** beyond mechanical selection (trade, region, win history as
   published). Scoring or enriching individuals from other sources would tip
   the balancing (DSK OH 1.3.2 b).

The intuition under test — "everything is public anyway, so why would consent
be needed?" — is half right for the wrong reason. Public availability does
**not** free data from the GDPR at all (finding 1); what actually removes the
consent requirement is that the GDPR never required consent for this kind of
processing in the first place — it requires *a* legal basis, and Art. 6(1)(f)
is one (finding 2).

## Findings

### 1. "Publicly available" does not escape the GDPR

Nothing in Art. 6 or anywhere else in the regulation exempts published personal
data; a legal basis is needed for every processing operation regardless of
source. The EDPB states it in one sentence, with three CJEU judgments behind
it: "The fact that personal data have been manifestly made public does not
automatically mean that they may be processed under Article 6(1)(f) GDPR …
However, this can be a factor to be taken into account when performing a
balancing test" ([EDPB Guidelines 1/2024](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2024-10/edpb_guidelines_202401_legitimateinterest_en.pdf),
footnote 52, citing CJEU C‑13/16 *Rīgas satiksme* para. 32, C‑708/18 para. 54,
and C‑468/10/C‑469/10 *ASNEF* para. 44).

So public availability is a *balancing factor*, not an exemption — and the DSK
shows the factor can even point the other way when publication was compelled
(finding 3).

### 2. Consent is not the required basis; Art. 6(1)(f) + Recital 47 carry postal B2B marketing

[Art. 6(1)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-6-gdpr/) lists consent (lit. a) and
legitimate interest (lit. f) as coequal legal bases. [Recital
47](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-47/) closes with: "The processing of
personal data for direct marketing purposes may be regarded as carried out for
a legitimate interest."

The DSK builds its whole direct-marketing guidance on that sentence: absent
consent, the basis for direct marketing is the Art. 6(1)(f) balancing (DSK OH
1.2: "Grundlage … ist … eine Interessenabwägung nach Art. 6 Abs. 1 UAbs. 1
lit. f DS-GVO", quoting Recital 47). For the *form* of contact, the DSK folds
the UWG's valuations into the balancing: if a channel is not permitted under
[§7 UWG](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/uwg_2004/__7.html), the legitimate
interest already fails (DSK OH 1.4). §7 Abs. 2 UWG requires prior express
consent for phone (toward consumers), automated calling machines, fax and
electronic mail — **postal letters are not on that list**; they are barred only
where the recipient's objection is recognisable (§7 Abs. 1 UWG). The EDPB says
the same from the ePrivacy side: "communications for the purpose of direct
marketing which are not provided by electronic means of communication (e.g., a
letter) are not covered by the material scope of the ePrivacy Directive and
would therefore not require consent under that Directive" (Guidelines 1/2024
para. 117), and notes that less intrusive campaigns — the example is sending
the same catalogue to a defined group — are exactly where Art. 6(1)(f) is
easiest to justify (para. 120).

The DSK's own worked example points the same way: postal advertising to a list
without profiling — even selected by a criterion such as postcode, as long as
the selection yields no additional insight about the person — "wird die
Interessenabwägung in der Regel … zugunsten des Verantwortlichen ausfallen"
(DSK OH 1.3.2 a). The moment selection becomes profiling (scores, behavioural
prediction, enrichment from external sources), the balancing flips and consent
becomes necessary (DSK OH 1.3.2 b).

Both regulators add the same caveat: Recital 47 is not a blank cheque. Direct
marketing "does not … always constitute a legitimate interest" (EDPB para.
110); the three-step test must actually be run and can fail, in particular
where data subjects "cannot reasonably expect that their data are used for
direct marketing purposes" (EDPB para. 112).

### 3. Purpose limitation: published for procurement contact, not for advertising

[Art. 5(1)(b)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-5-gdpr/) requires data to be "collected
for specified, explicit and legitimate purposes and not further processed in a
manner that is incompatible with those purposes." When data are to be used for
advertising although they were not (also) collected for advertising, the DSK
requires a compatibility check along [Art. 6(4)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-6-gdpr/)
(DSK OH 1.5). The Art. 6(4) factors: link between the purposes, the context of
collection and the relationship between data subject and controller, the
nature of the data, possible consequences for the data subject, and safeguards.

Two passages set the boundaries of what this means for us:

- **Against us — DSK OH 4.2, the closest regulator statement on point.**
  Harvesting contact data from a website's Impressum for marketing is
  inadmissible *although the data are publicly accessible*, because they were
  published under a legal obligation (§5 TMG / §18 MStV), not voluntarily:
  "Mangels Freiwilligkeit der Veröffentlichung führt die Interessenabwägung
  gem. Art. 6 Abs. 1 UAbs. 1 lit. f DS-GVO regelmäßig dazu, dass die werbliche
  Nutzung so erhobener Daten unzulässig ist." Contact details in TED and
  German award notices are likewise published because procurement law compels
  it. The analogy is not perfect — an Impressum is the operator's own legally
  forced self-disclosure to everyone, whereas an award notice names a firm
  that voluntarily entered a public, regulated bidding process whose publicity
  is part of the deal — but any honest LIA must quote DSK OH 4.2 and
  distinguish it, not ignore it.
- **For us — reasonable expectations are assessed in context.** Recital 47
  makes the balancing turn on "whether a data subject can reasonably expect at
  the time and in the context of the collection" the further processing; the
  EDPB lists the relevant contextual elements — existence and proximity of a
  relationship, place and context of collection, nature of the service, and
  whether the person would expect marketing *for such products* (Guidelines
  1/2024 paras. 50–54, 121). The offer here is unusually close to the context
  of publication: the recipients demonstrably bid on public tenders, and the
  product is finding public tenders. That is the "konkreter und aus dem
  Interessenbereich des Anzurufenden herzuleitender Grund" pattern the DSK
  itself uses (for B2B phone contact) as what lifts an approach above mere
  Sachbezogenheit (DSK OH 1.4.2 b). It strengthens the balancing; it does not
  remove the need to run it. Note also EDPB para. 53: fulfilling the Art. 13/14
  information duties does *not* by itself create reasonable expectations — a
  privacy notice cannot talk the balancing into passing (same point in DSK OH
  1.3.1).

### 4. Art. 14: what the people on the list must be told, and when

Because the data were **not obtained from the data subjects**, [Art.
14](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-14-gdpr/) applies, not Art. 13. Content (Art.
14(1)–(2)): controller identity and contact details, purposes and legal basis,
**the legitimate interests pursued** (14(2)(b)), categories of data, recipients,
storage period, the data subject rights including objection, the right to
complain to a supervisory authority, and **the source of the data**
(14(2)(f) — i.e. "taken from the published award notice of …"; the DSK counts
naming the source among fair-processing basics, DSK OH 1.3.1).

Timing (Art. 14(3)): within one month of obtaining the data at the latest —
but if the data "are to be used for communication with the data subject, at
the latest **at the time of the first communication** to that data subject"
(Art. 14(3)(b)), whichever comes first. The DSK spells out what that means for
a letter campaign: the information must come at the latest with the first
advertising mailing, and if it does, "sind beide Bestandteile (Information und
Werbetext) klar voneinander zu trennen und die Information (einschließlich des
Hinweises auf das Werbewiderspruchsrecht) entsprechend deutlich
herauszustellen" (DSK OH 2.2). **A notice only on the landing page is
therefore not enough** — the QR code is an offer to read more, not delivery of
the information; the notice belongs in or with the first letter itself, and
since the letter goes out within days of the list being compiled, the
one-month outer bound is met by the same act.

The Art. 14(5)(b) "disproportionate effort" exemption does not help here: the
regulation ties it "in particular" to archiving, research and statistical
purposes under Art. 89 safeguards, and its logic — you cannot reach the people
— collapses when the entire point of the processing is to post each person a
letter. Informing costs one paragraph in an envelope we are already paying
for.

### 5. Art. 21(2): the objection is unconditional and permanent

[Art. 21(2)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-21-gdpr/): the data subject may object
"at any time" to processing for direct marketing. Art. 21(3): after objection
the data "shall no longer be processed for such purposes" — no balancing, no
"compelling legitimate grounds" escape; the EDPB is explicit that this right
is "unconditional and irrespective of the legal basis relied on" and must be
free and easy to exercise (Guidelines 1/2024 para. 122). Art. 21(4) requires
the right to be brought to the person's attention "at the latest at the time
of the first communication … clearly and separately from any other
information" — the DSK recommends carrying the notice on *every* mailing and
says hiding it in AGB or long marketing copy does not count (DSK OH 5.2).
Implementation must be immediate: once an objection arrives, no new processing
(the DSK's example is precisely "neue Druckaufträge") may start (DSK OH 5.4).

The project's "do not contact" ledger flag, honoured forever, is exactly what
the DSK describes as a **Werbesperrdatei**: keeping the minimal contact data
*in order to keep suppressing* is itself lawful under Art. 21(3), Art. 6(1)(f)
and Art. 17(3)(b) — and, pointedly, a suppression list only works at all when
the marketing runs on Art. 6(1)(f), because consent-based marketing would need
per-case consent anyway (DSK OH 5.1). The person must be told, when their
objection is confirmed, why their data stays in the suppression file (DSK OH
5.1).

### 6. Scope: how much of the list is personal data at all

[Recital 14](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-14/): "This Regulation does not
cover the processing of personal data which concerns legal persons …,
including the name and the form of the legal person and the contact details of
the legal person." So:

- **Out of scope:** "Müller Blitzschutz GmbH", its registered address,
  `info@mueller-blitzschutz.de`. A letter addressed "Müller Blitzschutz GmbH,
  z. Hd. Geschäftsführung" processes no personal data. The EDPB notes the same
  boundary from the ePrivacy side (direct marketing addressed to legal
  persons, Guidelines 1/2024 para. 117).
- **In scope:** sole traders (e.K., Einzelunternehmen), GbR partners named in
  the winner string, any named natural-person contact
  ("Ansprechpartner: Herr Weber"), and personal e-mail addresses
  (`h.weber@…`) — for these, everything above applies in full.

For a mixed list the practical consequence runs in one direction: since the
awards store cannot always tell a GmbH from an e.K. reliably, and one
Einzelunternehmer row makes the pipeline a personal-data pipeline, **treat the
whole list as if the GDPR applies** — one regime, one notice text, one
suppression mechanism — while preferring the out-of-scope form where there is
a choice (address the firm and the role, not the named person; use the
published firm address, not someone's personal e-mail). That preference is
also Art. 5(1)(c) data minimisation doing balancing work (EDPB para. 119).

### 7. The documented assessment, and the Art. 30 record

The LIA is not optional paperwork: Art. 5(2) makes the controller "able to
demonstrate compliance", and the EDPB requires that controllers "carefully
assess and **document** whether these three cumulative conditions are met …
**before** carrying out the relevant processing operations" (Guidelines 1/2024,
executive summary and para. 9 ff.). The three cumulative conditions (para. 112,
following CJEU C‑252/21 *Meta* para. 106):

1. **Purpose** — a legitimate interest: lawful, clearly and precisely
   articulated, real and present, not speculative (para. 17). Here: acquiring
   customers among firms that demonstrably win public contracts, for a product
   that monitors public contracts.
2. **Necessity** — no less intrusive means that achieves the interest as
   effectively, and data minimisation respected (para. 119). Here: name,
   firm, address, won-lot references; postal channel; no enrichment.
3. **Balancing** — interests and rights of the data subjects do not override,
   assessed concretely per campaign, not abstractly (DSK OH 1.3.1: "Ein bloßes
   Abstellen auf abstrakte oder auf vergleichbare Fälle … genügt … nicht"),
   including reasonable expectations (finding 3), the DSK OH 4.2 problem, and
   the safeguards that tip it: postal only, no profiling, notice in letter,
   instant permanent opt-out.

Separately, [Art. 30(1)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-30-gdpr/) requires a record
of processing activities naming purposes, categories of data subjects and
data, recipients, envisaged erasure time limits and security measures. The
under-250-employees exemption of Art. 30(5) does not apply, because it falls
away where "the processing is not occasional" — a standing target list worked
through in batches is not occasional. The target-list processing (and the
suppression file as its own entry) must appear in that record.

## What this project must actually do

- **Write the LIA** before the first batch: the three steps above, quoting and
  distinguishing DSK OH 4.2, naming the safeguards. It is the document the
  lawyer reviews — decision #1 in `ONBOARDING.md` §7 stays open until they do.
- **Put the Art. 14 notice in the first letter**: controller identity, purpose,
  legal basis Art. 6(1)(f) and the interest pursued, source ("published award
  notice, TED/…"), storage period, rights — visually separate from the pitch,
  with the Art. 21 objection right separately and prominently stated. The
  landing page carries the long version; the letter carries the notice.
- **Honour objections immediately and forever**: any channel, no form
  requirements, no reasons asked; flag in the ledger before the next print
  run starts; keep the minimal suppression entry and say why.
- **Add the processing to the Art. 30 record** (target list + suppression
  file).
- **Treat the whole list as in-scope**: sole traders and named contact persons
  are personal data; prefer firm-and-role addressing over named persons
  wherever the notice data allows.
- **Keep selection mechanical**: trade, region, published wins. No scoring of
  persons, no enrichment from other sources — that is the line between DSK OH
  1.3.2 a (fine) and 1.3.2 b (consent).

## Caveat

This is research into the regulation text and regulator guidance (DSK, EDPB),
done to shrink the lawyer question to its actual core — the DSK OH 4.2
balancing point — not to answer it. The EDPB guidelines cited are the version
put to public consultation in October 2024. None of this is legal advice, and
`ONBOARDING.md` §7 decision #1 stands as written: a lawyer signs off on the
legal basis before the first batch goes out.
